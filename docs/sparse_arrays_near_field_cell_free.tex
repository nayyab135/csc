\section{Where your work stands, and what a Transactions paper needs}
\label{sec:context}

\textbf{ICASSP paper.} Cross-AP list-based SIC for hybrid near-/far-field cell-free XL-MIMO with \emph{perfect} CSI \cite{haider2026icassp}: the candidate list is formed from both the cluster-fused and the all-AP fused soft estimates, cancellation is network-wide, and an LDPC-coded IDD scheme is added.

\textbf{VTC paper.} The same detector with \emph{estimated} CSI from polar-domain channel estimation over all AP--UE pairs \cite{haider2026vtc,cui2022channel}.

\textbf{What a Transactions paper needs.} A new technical dimension (not only a merge of the two conference papers), analysis, and a broad numerical study. Sparse APs are a strong candidate, because they change three things your papers rely on:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{where} the near field is: the Rayleigh distance grows with the square of the aperture \cite{lu2024tutorial,bjornson2021primer};
  \item \emph{how well} co-angular users can be separated in range: resolution improves with the aperture \cite{chen2024nearfield,wang2024sparsemimo,zhou2025sparse};
  \item \emph{what goes wrong}: grating lobes \cite{zhou2025sparse,chen2025exploring,chen2024nearfield}. Your cross-AP fusion is a natural way to fix this (Section~\ref{sec:why}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{What is a sparse array?}
\label{sec:what}

A conventional uniform linear array (ULA) has spacing $d=\lambda/2$. A \textbf{sparse array} uses spacing larger than $\lambda/2$, or non-uniform positions \cite{zhou2025sparse,chen2024nearfield,li2024sparseisac}.

\paragraph{Main families.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Uniform sparse ULA} (``linear sparse array'' (LSA) in \cite{zhou2025sparse}, ``uniform sparse array'' (USA) in \cite{chen2024nearfield}). Spacing $d=\eta\,\lambda/2$ with integer sparsity factor $\eta>1$; \cite{zhou2025sparse} writes $U$ and \cite{chen2024nearfield} writes $p$ for this factor. Positions:
  \begin{equation}
    p_n=\Big(n-\tfrac{N+1}{2}\Big)\,\eta\,\frac{\lambda}{2},\qquad n=1,\dots,N.
    \label{eq:pos}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Thinned array}: a dense array in which only a subset of elements is active. The aperture stays the same with fewer RF chains; the active set can be reconfigured \cite{hussain2026thinning}.
  \item \textbf{Non-uniform sparse arrays}: minimum-redundancy \cite{moffet1968mra}, nested \cite{pal2010nested} and co-prime \cite{vaidyanathan2011coprime} arrays, the extended co-prime array (ECA) of \cite{zhou2025sparse}, and position-optimised arrays \cite{chen2024nearfield}. Non-uniform spacing removes the exact periodicity that causes grating lobes \cite{chen2024nearfield,zhou2025sparse}.
  \item \textbf{Modular arrays}: dense sub-arrays separated by large gaps \cite{li2023modular,kosasih2025mla}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Two ways to compare with a dense array.}
These give different conclusions, so always state which one you use:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Same number of antennas $N$}: the sparse array has a larger aperture. This is the setting of \cite{wang2024sparsemimo,zhou2025sparse} (``given the same number of array elements'').
  \item \textbf{Same aperture}: about $N/\eta$ antennas, which is the motivation of thinning \cite{hussain2026thinning} and of modular arrays with a limited number of antennas \cite{kosasih2025mla}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why sparse arrays matter in the near field: numbers for your system}
\label{sec:why}

\subsection{The near-field region grows with $\eta^2$}
With aperture $D=(N-1)d$, the Rayleigh distance used in your papers (eq.~(2) of \cite{haider2026vtc}, following \cite{lu2024tutorial}) is $R=2D^2/\lambda$. With $d=\eta\lambda/2$:
\begin{equation}
  R(\eta)=\frac{(N-1)^2\eta^2\lambda}{2}=\eta^2\,R(1)\qquad\text{(derived here from $R=2D^2/\lambda$).}
  \label{eq:rayleigh}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Doubling the spacing makes the near-field region four times larger.}

\subsection{Range focusing needs the user well inside $R$}
Being inside $R$ is not enough for a sharp focus in distance.
\begin{itemize}
  \item For a \emph{planar} array focusing on its axis, Bj\"ornson \emph{et al.} prove that the 3-dB depth of focus is finite only if the focal distance $F<d_{\rm FA}/10$, where $d_{\rm FA}=2D_{\rm diag}^2/\lambda$ uses the array diagonal \cite[eq.~(17), Theorem~1, eq.~(23)]{bjornson2021primer}.
  \item For a \emph{linear} (dense or sparse) array, Zhou \emph{et al.} give the same structure \cite[Proposition~2, eq.~(22)]{zhou2025sparse}:
  \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{BD}_{3\,\rm dB}=
    \begin{cases}
      \dfrac{2r_0^2\,r_{\rm th}}{r_{\rm th}^2-r_0^2}, & r_0<r_{\rm th},\\[6pt]
      \infty, & r_0\ge r_{\rm th},
    \end{cases}
    \qquad
    r_{\rm th}\approx\frac{Q^2U^2d_0\cos^2\theta_0}{4\beta_{3\rm dB}^2},\quad \beta_{3\rm dB}=1.6,
    \label{eq:depth}
  \end{equation}
  with $Q$ antennas, sparsity $U$ (our $\eta$), $d_0=\lambda/2$ and user angle $\theta_0$.
\end{itemize}
Since $R\approx Q^2U^2d_0$ for a ULA with $d_0=\lambda/2$, \eqref{eq:depth} gives (derived here)
\begin{equation}
  r_{\rm th}\approx\frac{R\cos^2\theta_0}{10.24},
  \label{eq:rth}
\end{equation}
which matches the $d_{\rm FA}/10$ rule of \cite{bjornson2021primer}, and adds the factor $\cos^2\theta_0$: focusing weakens towards end-fire and disappears at $\theta_0=\pm90^\circ$ \cite[Remark~1]{zhou2025sparse}. Zhou \emph{et al.} also note that a higher sparsity gives a more prominent focusing effect \cite[Remark~1]{zhou2025sparse}.

\begin{keybox}{What Table~\ref{tab:numbers} tells you about your current papers}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Each user in your simulations is placed 20--150\,m from its anchor AP (other APs are farther). With compact APs ($\eta=1$), $r_{\rm th}\approx19$\,m at broadside, so \textbf{almost no link has a finite beam depth}. The links are near-field by the Rayleigh criterion of \cite{haider2026vtc}, but two co-angular users at 30\,m and 120\,m still have correlation 0.83.
  \item With the \emph{same 64 antennas} at $\eta=2$ the correlation drops to 0.36, and at $\eta=4$ to 0.15. This agrees with the finding that larger spacing improves spatial resolution and multi-user sum rate \cite{chen2024nearfield,wang2024sparsemimo}.
  \item With the \emph{same aperture} (rows 4--5) the correlation is unchanged: range resolution depends on the aperture, not on the number of antennas. A thinned array gives the same separation with $2\times$ or $4\times$ fewer antennas, which is the motivation of \cite{hussain2026thinning}.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\subsection{More degrees of freedom}
Wang \emph{et al.} define the effective degrees of freedom (EDoF) of a channel $\mathbf H$ as \cite[eq.~(13)]{wang2024sparsemimo}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{EDoF}=\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf H\mathbf H^{\mathsf H})}{\|\mathbf H\mathbf H^{\mathsf H}\|_F}\right)^2,
  \label{eq:edof}
\end{equation}
and prove, for single-user near-field sparse MIMO, a closed-form EDoF that increases with the array sparsity until it reaches $\min(N_t,N_r)$ \cite{wang2024sparsemimo}. Chen \emph{et al.} give a constraint under which the EDoF of sparse UPAs can be estimated in closed form \cite{chen2025exploring}. For multi-user detection, the analogous effect is a better-conditioned local channel matrix $\mathbf H_l$, i.e.\ the rank enhancement of eq.~(10) in \cite{haider2026vtc}.

\subsection{Grating lobes: the price, and what the near field changes}
\label{sec:gl}
A uniform sparse ULA repeats its beam in $\sin\theta$ with period $2/\eta$. Its grating lobes are at \cite[Lemma~5, eq.~(23)]{zhou2025sparse}
\begin{equation}
  \sin\theta_u=\sin\theta_0+\frac{2u}{\eta},\qquad |\sin\theta_u|\le1,
  \label{eq:glangle}
\end{equation}
and there are $2(\eta-1)$ of them over the whole angular range, each with beam height 1 (as strong as the main lobe) \cite[Lemma~5]{zhou2025sparse}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Far field}: the grating lobes have the same peak power as the main lobe, so there is no suppression \cite[Sec.~IV, after Corollary~3]{chen2025exploring}.
  \item \textbf{Near field, at the focal distance}: the grating lobes are suppressed, and more so for shorter focal distance and larger spacing \cite[Corollary~3, eq.~(40)]{chen2025exploring}. The same authors warn that ``particular attention should be paid to the few grating lobes that retain relatively high power'' \cite[Sec.~IV]{chen2025exploring}. The near-field model also suppresses the grating lobes of modular arrays better than the far-field model \cite{li2023modular}.
  \item \textbf{Near field, at a shifted range}: the grating lobe is \emph{refocused} at another distance, where it is again as strong as the main lobe. Zhou \emph{et al.} show that at the grating-lobe angle the beam pattern depends on $\cos^2\theta_0/r_0-\cos^2\theta_u/r$ \cite[Lemma~6, eq.~(24)]{zhou2025sparse}, which vanishes at
  \begin{equation}
    r'=r_0\,\frac{\cos^2\theta_u}{\cos^2\theta_0}=r_0\,\frac{1-\sin^2\theta_u}{1-\sin^2\theta_0}
    \label{eq:rprime}
  \end{equation}
  (rewritten here from \cite[eq.~(24)]{zhou2025sparse}). Zhou \emph{et al.} describe these lobes as ``focused on specific regions'' with power comparable to the main lobe \cite[Abstract, Remark~2]{zhou2025sparse}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example computed here} (your array, $\eta=2$, focus at $\sin\theta_0=0.3$, $r_0=40$\,m): gain 0.38 at the grating-lobe angle $\sin\theta_u=-0.7$ at the same range; gain 0.89 at the same angle at $r'=22.4$\,m from \eqref{eq:rprime}; gain 1.00 for a far-field user at that angle.

\begin{ideabox}{Why this is good news for a cell-free paper (proposed here)}
In a hybrid-field cell-free network each AP sees a user at a different angle and range, so the ambiguity point \eqref{eq:rprime} of a user is different at every AP. Two users that collide on a grating lobe at one AP are unlikely to collide at all APs. A detector that fuses \emph{all} APs, like your cross-AP list SIC \cite{haider2026icassp,haider2026vtc}, can then resolve what one AP, or the serving cluster, cannot. Sparse APs should therefore make the information surplus $\Delta_k$ of your papers (eq.~(26) of \cite{haider2026vtc}) larger. This is a hypothesis to prove; no paper found in this search has studied it.
\end{ideabox}

\section{Rules and conditions to respect}
\label{sec:rules}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{R\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{State the comparison basis}: same $N$ \cite{wang2024sparsemimo,zhou2025sparse} or same aperture \cite{hussain2026thinning,kosasih2025mla}.
  \item \textbf{Recompute the near/far-field boundary.} $R\propto\eta^2$ \eqref{eq:rayleigh}. In your $300\times300$\,m$^2$ area, $\eta=2$ gives $R=794$\,m, so almost every link becomes near-field and the \emph{hybrid} regime disappears (computed here). Keep a hybrid network with a larger area, with mixed APs, or with a boundary that depends on angle as well \cite{lu2024tutorial,stutz2026boundaries}.
  \item \textbf{Range focusing needs $r_0<r_{\rm th}$} \eqref{eq:depth}--\eqref{eq:rth} \cite{zhou2025sparse,bjornson2021primer}. Report the fraction of links inside $r_{\rm th}$, and remember the $\cos^2\theta_0$ factor.
  \item \textbf{Account for grating lobes everywhere}: inter-user correlation, clustering, and any use of estimated angles. Far-field links of a sparse AP see grating lobes at full strength \cite{chen2025exploring}.
  \item \textbf{Estimation dictionary.} The angular grid must be about $\eta$ times finer, because the main lobe narrows to a null-to-null width of $4/(N\eta)$ in $\sin\theta$ \cite[Proposition~1]{zhou2025sparse}. For one AP, the channel vector is periodic in the surrogate-distance/angle domain with period $2/\eta$, so a dictionary over one period is enough to estimate the \emph{vector} \cite[Sec.~IV-A, eq.~(47)]{chen2024nearfield}; see Section~\ref{sec:est} for what this means for angle and range.
  \item \textbf{Keep the element model consistent.} In your code the far-field correlation matrix uses the spacing in wavelengths (\texttt{d\_H = 0.5}); it must become $\eta/2$ together with the NUSW positions (code check, done here).
  \item \textbf{Hardware and channel assumptions.} A larger aperture raises cost, size and spatial non-stationarity concerns; thinning keeps the hardware but switches elements \cite{hussain2026thinning}. Wideband operation makes grating lobes move with frequency \cite{li2024sparseisac}; your system is narrowband, so state it. Recent near-field cell-free work uses dense planar arrays with LoS and NLoS components \cite{wang2026combining}; your model is a ULA with LoS (near-field) and correlated Rayleigh (far-field) links, so state the difference.
  \item \textbf{Non-uniform arrays} (ECA, position-optimised, nested, co-prime) reduce the grating-lobe peaks: the ECA ``can greatly suppress the beam power of near-field grating-lobes'' but has more of them \cite[Abstract, Sec.~III]{zhou2025sparse}, and optimised non-uniform arrays have no grating lobes in the SD-A domain \cite[Sec.~IV-A]{chen2024nearfield}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical background}
\label{sec:math}

\subsection{Channel model with a sparse AP}
Keep the NUSW model of your papers (eq.~(6) of \cite{haider2026vtc}, from \cite{lu2024tutorial}) and change only the positions \eqref{eq:pos}:
\begin{equation}
  [\h^{\rm NF}_{l,k}]_n=\sqrt{\beta_{l,k}}\,\frac{d_{l,k}}{d_{n,l,k}}\,
  e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{n,l,k}},\qquad
  d_{n,l,k}=\sqrt{d_{l,k}^2+p_n^2-2\,d_{l,k}\,p_n\sin\theta_{l,k}}.
\end{equation}
The far-field model becomes $\sqrt{\beta_{l,k}}\,\mathbf a(\theta_{l,k})$ with $[\mathbf a(\theta)]_n=e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}p_n\sin\theta}$ (eqs.~(8)--(9) of \cite{haider2026vtc} with the new $p_n$).

\subsection{Fresnel form, surrogate distance and array gain}
The second-order expansion $d_n\approx r-p_n s+\frac{p_n^2(1-s^2)}{2r}$, $s=\sin\theta$, is eq.~(7) of \cite{haider2026vtc} and the basis of the polar-domain model of \cite{cui2022channel}. Chen \emph{et al.} call
\begin{equation}
  b=\frac{1-s^2}{2r}
  \label{eq:surrogate}
\end{equation}
the \emph{surrogate distance} \cite[eq.~(6)]{chen2024nearfield}. With it, the unit-norm steering vector is
\begin{equation}
  [\bb(s,b)]_n\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\exp\!\Big(j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\big(p_n s-p_n^2\,b\big)\Big),
  \label{eq:steer}
\end{equation}
and the normalized gain when focusing at $(s_0,b_0)$ and looking at $(s,b)$ is (derived here from \eqref{eq:steer})
\begin{equation}
  G(s,b)=\Big|\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N
  \exp\!\Big(j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\big[p_n(s-s_0)-p_n^2(b-b_0)\big]\Big)\Big|^2 .
  \label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Grating lobes in the near field}
\label{sec:gl-math}
With \eqref{eq:pos}, the linear phase in \eqref{eq:gain} is a multiple of $2\pi$ for every $n$ when $s-s_0=2u/\eta$, which gives \eqref{eq:glangle} \cite[Lemma~5]{zhou2025sparse}. At those angles only the quadratic term remains:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $b=b_0$, the quadratic phase vanishes and the gain is 1. In physical coordinates this is \eqref{eq:rprime} \cite[Lemma~6]{zhou2025sparse}. In the $(s,b)$ domain, the focus pattern is therefore exactly periodic with period $2/\eta$ \cite[Sec.~IV-A]{chen2024nearfield}.
  \item At the same physical range $r=r_0$, $b-b_0=(s_0^2-s_u^2)/(2r_0)$. The largest quadratic phase, at the array edge $p=D/2$, is (derived here)
  \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\max}=\frac{\pi R}{8\,r_0}\,\big|s_u^2-s_0^2\big|,
  \end{equation}
  so the lobe is strongly attenuated when $r_0\lesssim R|s_u^2-s_0^2|/8$. Since $R\propto\eta^2$, sparser arrays and closer users give more suppression, consistent with \cite[Corollary~3]{chen2025exploring}.
  \item As $r_0\to\infty$, $b_0\to0$ and $\Phi_{\max}\to0$: no suppression in the far field \cite{chen2025exploring}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Inter-user correlation and resolvability}
At AP $l$ define $\mu_l(i,j)=|\h_{l,i}^{\mathsf H}\h_{l,j}|/(\|\h_{l,i}\|\,\|\h_{l,j}\|)$. Users $i,j$ are unresolvable at AP $l$ when $\mu_l(i,j)\approx1$, for example when one sits at the other's ambiguity point \eqref{eq:rprime}. A network-level measure for a set of APs $\mathcal T$ is (proposed here)
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\rm net}(i,j;\mathcal T)=\min_{l\in\mathcal T}\mu_l(i,j).
\end{equation}
The difference between $\mathcal T=\mathcal S_k$ (cluster) and $\mathcal T=\{1,\dots,L\}$ is what cross-AP detection exploits. For a single uniform sparse array, Chen \emph{et al.} show that the total coverage of the main lobes (including grating lobes) is still no larger than that of a dense half-wavelength ULA with the same number of antennas, so the sparse array has higher spatial resolution despite grating lobes \cite[Property~3]{chen2024nearfield}.

\subsection{Effect on your cross-AP information surplus}
Your papers define the per-AP SINR $\gamma_{l,k}$ (eq.~(23) of \cite{haider2026vtc}) and the surplus (eq.~(26) of \cite{haider2026vtc})
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k\approx
  \frac{\sum_{l\notin\mathcal S_k}\gamma_{l,k}}{\big(1+\sum_{l\in\mathcal S_k}\gamma_{l,k}\big)\big(1+\sum_{l=1}^L\gamma_{l,k}\big)} .
\end{equation}
With sparse APs, $\gamma_{l,k}$ collapses at APs where user $k$ sits on an ambiguity point of a strong interferer and stays large elsewhere. When such an AP is in the cluster, $\Delta_k$ grows (hypothesis proposed here). A bound on $\mathbb E[\Delta_k]$ as a function of $\eta$ would be a Transactions-level result.

\section{The gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Within the limits of this search:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sparse arrays in the near field are studied for \textbf{single arrays}: beam patterns, EDoF, precoding, thinning, estimation and sensing \cite{chen2025exploring,chen2024nearfield,zhou2025sparse,wang2024sparsemimo,hussain2026thinning,li2024sparseisac,li2023modular,kosasih2025mla}.
  \item Near-field \textbf{cell-free} work uses dense arrays and studies combining, activity detection or downlink focusing \cite{wang2026combining,lei2026activity,lei2023uplink,mylonopoulos2025los}. Only \cite{xie2025beamfocusing} mentions sparse arrays, for downlink beam focusing.
  \item \textbf{No work was found} on uplink data detection (SIC, list detection, IDD) or channel estimation for \emph{hybrid-field cell-free} networks with \emph{sparse APs}.
  \item \textbf{No work was found} that uses \emph{multi-AP fusion} to resolve per-AP grating-lobe ambiguities. Kosasih \emph{et al.} fuse sub-array estimates within one modular array \cite{kosasih2025mla}, which is the closest idea.
\end{enumerate}
Point 4 is the strongest claim, because it connects directly to the cross-AP idea of your papers. ``Not found'' means ``not found by this search''.

\section{Channel estimation with sparse APs (connects to your VTC paper)}
\label{sec:est}

Three points, the second one corrected in this version:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Finer angular grid.} Use about $\eta$ times more angle samples, because the main lobe is $\eta$ times narrower \cite[Proposition~1]{zhou2025sparse}. Sample the range in the surrogate distance \eqref{eq:surrogate} \cite{chen2024nearfield} or in $1/r$ per angle \cite{cui2022channel}.
  \item \textbf{Periodicity: harmless for the channel vector, harmful for parameters.} For one uniform sparse AP, $\bb(s,b)$ and $\bb(s+2/\eta,b)$ are nearly identical \eqref{eq:steer}. Chen \emph{et al.} therefore sample the angle over a single period $[-1/p,1/p]$ \cite[eq.~(47)]{chen2024nearfield}: the estimated \emph{channel vector} is correct even if the angle is aliased. The aliasing only matters when the estimated angle and range are \emph{used}, for example for localization, to combine information across APs, or in a clustering rule based on geometry. (The authentic estimator in your MATLAB code resolves a related end-fire alias for $\eta=1$, where $s=-1$ and $s=+1$ coincide.)
  \item \textbf{Cross-AP location-domain refinement (proposed here).} A user has one position $\mathbf u_k$, which fixes $(\theta_{l,k},d_{l,k})$ at every AP. Estimating $\mathbf u_k$ jointly from all near-field APs removes the per-AP aliases, because an alias consistent with AP $l$ is not consistent with AP $l'$. This extends to cell-free the per-sub-array fusion by least-squares triangulation of \cite{kosasih2025mla}. Off-grid refinement can follow \cite{chen2024nearfield,cui2022channel}.
\end{enumerate}
